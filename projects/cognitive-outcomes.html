Measuring cognitive outcomes under real-world conditions

I'm interested in what learners actually do when real-life conditions diverge from training. If the novel test input is noisier, more variable, or otherwise unlike what a learner has seen before, what kind of representation supports recognition, and how do we measure it?

The core question

What does a learner's performance under shifted conditions tell us about the representations they're using? How do we design methodologically rigorous studies that distinguish true generalization from spurious pattern matching?

Prior experience as a feature attribution problem

The same input can produce different learning outcomes depending on what each learner treats as informative. In an artificial-language study I completed as an honors thesis in 2014 with Mandarin and English speakers, both groups heard identical streams of disyllabic nonsense words paired with tone contours. Mandarin speakers segmented words better when the tones were consistent than when they were random. English speakers performed identically across conditions. They were not using the tonal cue at all.

This is a feature attribution problem, in which we attempt to identify which specific input dimension a learner is using to solve a task. Tsui et al. (2021), building on my honors thesis, extends the same logic to infants segmenting two interleaved artificial languages, where phonetic cues determine whether the streams can be tracked separately. Two later studies I conducted in graduate school show experience-driven divergence in domestic dogs: dogs distinguish their name from a vowel-mispronounced version but not a consonant-mispronounced version, and dogs from English- versus Spanish-speaking households produce opposite preference patterns to the same fairy tales.

Translation to applied learning settings

Mallikarjun, Newman, and Novick (2017) takes the same line of evidence into K-12 ELL practice. Common policies, particularly English-only immersion, rest on assumptions psycholinguistic evidence does not support: bilingual learners cannot "deactivate" their second language, cognates and partial cognates are coactivated automatically, and mixed-language instruction does not appear to harm learning outcomes and may actually help them.

Most existing policy guidance treats the bilingual learner as a monolingual with a delay. The paper argues for the opposite framing: the learner's existing language is a resource the learning environment can either exploit or actively work against. Designing for one or the other is a choice, and most current designs make it without realizing they're making it.

Cognitive control and language processing in adults

Two currently unpublished studies conducted at UMD extend the framework to adult populations. The first tests adults with and without ADHD on past-tense generation, task-switching, and working memory, treating stimulant medication as a within-subjects covariate rather than ignoring it.

The second is an EEG study completed in graduate school examining masked argument-structure priming. Native English speakers read garden-path sentences with a 33ms masked prime preceding the matrix verb, and primes varied continuously in their DO/SC bias rather than splitting into two categorical conditions. I built the EEGlab preprocessing pipeline and ran regression-based ERP analyses in R. The planned interaction was not significant, but visual inspection at the target verb showed earlier N400 peaks for repetition primes and larger P600 amplitudes for argument-structure primes, indicating that 33ms primes were being processed even though participants reported only seeing flashes.

What this work shows

  • Input can produce different outcomes depending on the experience of the learner. Beginning to identify what feature(s) each learner uses requires careful experimental design.

Relevance to ML and AI

The difficulty of understanding what features a learner uses and why directly relates to the current cutting-edge research using LLMs and nonsupervised ML algorithms. A model that performs well on its test set may or may not be using the features an experimenter or programmer intended, and may or may not generalize in an expected way when the test set changes. Interdisciplinary work leveraging a complex computational system (the dog brain) and our current complex computational AI systems can allow us to increase robustness in our algorithms.
