Evaluation frameworks for learned systems

I'm interested trained detectors can be evaluated and stress-tested prior to deployment. The detectors in this project is a set of working dogs trained to identify chronic wasting disease in white-tailed deer feces. However, the questions and methodology are very similar to those asked about any trained classifier: how can we build a safe training set, test for optimal generalization to real-world circumstances, and tell whether the system is using the features we think it is using?

The core question

What happens when a trained detector meets a sample it wasn't trained on, and how do you find out before deployment?

Substrate validation: to what extent do safer training aids align with the true material?

CWD-positive deer feces are a biohazard; prions bind to soil, persist for years, and survive routine decontamination. Training dogs on real fecal matter from CWD-positive deer risks contaminating every environment in which the training takes place. The first question I examined was the extent to which a safer training substrate can stand in for the real material without changing what the dog learns and their future operational success.

Cotton balls incubated in the headspace above fecal samples adsorb the volatile organic compounds (VOCs) the dogs are detecting, without ever contacting the feces themselves; thus, ,there is no prion risk. Dogs trained on cotton aids reached 73% sensitivity and 77% specificity on novel cotton aids incubated with never-before-seen fecal samples.

Dogs can generalize from cotton training aids to fecal matter in Chronic Wasting Disease detection (Mallikarjun et al., 2025)

Generalization across the training-to-deployment gap

However, the cotton aid isn't the operational target. Operational dogs deployed in the field will encounter real deer feces, in pile sizes that vary by an order of magnitude, and must alert on feces from CWD-positive deer. Two follow-up studies asked about the extent to which dogs trained on the safer substrate generalize to more operational odors.

From cotton aid incubated with 2g of fecal matter to the 2g of fecal matter itself, sensitivity dropped from 73% to 50%. Specificity held at 90%. Dogs recognized something in common across the two substrates, but the fecal samples sat at the edge of their learned concept rather than at its center.

However, deer typically produce fecal piles weighing about 22g. As such, we asked about dogs' generalization from the small-quantity odor to a larger quantity odor, using cotton aids. From cotton aids incubated with 1-2 g of fecal matter to aids incubated with 22g, the result was stark: dogs didn't spontaneously generalize at all. Performance on the larger-volume samples was indistinguishable from chance until I gave them three short training sessions on the new volume, at which point discrimination partially recovered. Even with the same fundamental target in the same substrate with only the sample mass changed, the trained representation didn't transfer.

Lab to field

The earlier proof-of-concept work tested three dogs on the same discrimination task in two settings: a scent wheel in a low-distraction laboratory room, and outdoor search areas in tall grass. In the lab, dogs alerted significantly more often on CWD-positive samples than on CWD-negative samples, with a false-alert rate of 8%. In the field, they located 8 of 11 positive samples, and the false-alert rate rose to 17%. The discrimination held; the context changed how often they made mistakes.

A second finding from the same study is worth pulling out. Handlers were asked, in real time, whether they thought their dog's alert was a true or false alert. They identified every true alert correctly, but endorsed 8 of 14 false alerts as correct. The human is part of the detection system, and their agreement level with the dog's classification is its own measurement problem.
