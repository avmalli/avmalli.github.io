Evaluation frameworks for learned systems

I'm interested in how you evaluate a trained detector before you deploy it. The detector in this work is a working dog trained to identify chronic wasting disease in white-tailed deer feces. The questions are the same ones that come up for any trained classifier: how do you build a safe training set, how do you test for generalization, and how do you tell whether the system is using the features you think it is?

The core question

What evidence do you need to trust a trained detector outside its training conditions, and what do you do when that evidence says the system isn't using the features you assumed it was?

Substrate validation: can a safer training aid carry the signal?

CWD-positive deer feces are a biohazard. Prions bind to soil, persist for years, and survive routine decontamination. Training dogs on real fecal matter risks contaminating every environment the training takes place in. So the first question I worked on was whether a safer training substrate can stand in for the real material without changing what the dog learns.

Cotton balls incubated in the headspace above fecal samples adsorb the volatile organic compounds (VOCs) the dogs are detecting, without ever contacting the feces themselves. I trained five dogs on cotton-ball aids prepared this way and tested them on novel aids and on real fecal matter. Dogs reached 73% sensitivity and 77% specificity on novel cotton aids, with no significant difference between cotton balls and the denser cotton rolls preferred for field use.

Generalization across the training-to-deployment gap

The cotton aid isn't the operational target. Dogs deployed in the field will encounter real deer feces, in pile sizes that vary by an order of magnitude. Two follow-up studies asked whether dogs trained on the safer substrate generalize to the operational one.

From cotton aid to real feces, sensitivity dropped from 73% to 50%. Specificity held at 90%. Dogs recognized something in common across the two substrates, but the fecal samples sat at the edge of their learned concept rather than at its center. From 1-2g cotton aids to 22g cotton aids, the result was sharper: dogs didn't spontaneously generalize at all. Performance on the larger-volume samples was indistinguishable from chance until I gave them three short training sessions on the new volume, at which point discrimination recovered. Same target, same substrate, only the sample mass changed, and the trained representation didn't transfer.

Lab to field

The earlier proof-of-concept work tested three dogs on the same discrimination task in two settings: a scent wheel in a low-distraction laboratory room, and outdoor search areas in tall grass. In the lab, dogs alerted significantly more often on CWD-positive samples than on CWD-negative samples, with a false-alert rate of 8%. In the field, they located 8 of 11 positive samples, and the false-alert rate rose to 17%. The discrimination held; the context changed how often they made mistakes.

A second finding from the same study is worth pulling out. Handlers were asked, in real time, whether they thought their dog's alert was a true or false alert. They identified every true alert correctly, but endorsed 8 of 14 false alerts as correct. The human in the loop is part of the system, and their agreement with the dog's classification is its own measurement problem.

What the chemistry told us about the dog

A USDA-funded arm of the program ran in parallel. Collaborators at Children's Hospital of Philadelphia ran two-dimensional gas chromatography mass spectrometry on the same fecal samples the dogs worked with, and identified ten VOCs that statistically discriminated CWD-positive from CWD-negative samples. A Random Forest trained on the full profile reached an AUC of 0.96.

We then ran the test that mattered for training-aid development. I synthesized "positive" aids by spiking CWD-negative feces with the three most predictive VOCs from that set, at concentrations matched to confirmed positive samples. If those three features were what the dogs were detecting, the spiked samples should have read as positive. They didn't. Dogs trained on real fecal matter classified the spiked samples as negative.

The result rules out the simplest hypothesis about what the dogs were using. The dogs are detecting something the top three statistical discriminators don't capture, whether that's a wider feature set, relative concentrations across many compounds, or interactions with the background matrix the spike couldn't reproduce. The negative result is more informative than the original GC-MS ranking was on its own: it tells us which hypothesis to drop.

What this work shows

  • Substrate, generalization, and feature attribution are separable questions, and they need separate experimental designs.
  • The trained distribution sets the generalization range. Shifts in substrate, sample volume, or deployment context all degrade performance.
  • GC-MS feature importance doesn't necessarily describe what the dog is using. An ablation can disagree with the chemistry, and the disagreement is informative.
  • When humans interpret a detector's output, their agreement rate with the system is its own measurement question.

Broader relevance

The questions transfer to any trained classifier you're deciding whether to deploy: how to build a training set that covers what the system will actually see, how to test for generalization before the cost of failure is borne in the field, how to design ablations that test what you think they test, and how to account for the humans interpreting the output.
