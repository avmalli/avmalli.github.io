A VLM case study examining visual canine body condition scoring

I evaluated four frontier vision-language models against a measured human expertise gradient on a visual ordinal task (canine body condition scoring). The models can then be directly compared to the human gradient. The methods generalize to any visual ordinal scale with a standardized scoring system, an expert benchmark, and a measurable gradient of human expertise.

Highlights

  • Claude Opus 4.7: no detected difference from any human group, including veterinary professionals.
  • GPT-5.5: no detected difference from any human group, including veterinary professionals.
  • Claude Sonnet 4.6: significantly worse than every veterinary professional group; performs like the non-veterinary group.
  • GPT-4.1: without reference material, significantly worse than every human group, even the non-veterinary group; with reference material, performs like the non-veterinary group.

Opus 4.7 / GPT 5.5's scores differ from Sonnet / GPT-4.1's scores by roughly the same amount that a veterinarian differs from a member of the public.

Why a measured gradient

Model evaluation against human performance usually takes one of two forms: either the comparison is against a small expert panel, or against a large unstratified group of people. This study allows examination of the specific place that different models sit on a human expertise gradient, and how well different models reflect human behavior in the grading task.

I collected a large dataset from experts with various levels of experience and non-experts in an ordinal grading task (body condition scoring of dogs), and used this dataset to evaluate four frontier vision-language models. The methods generalize to any visual ordinal clinical scale. The dataset collection was funded by the Waltham Foundation.

The human anchor

Body condition scoring (BCS) is a routine veterinary assessment. A trained clinician visually examines the dog and palpates the dog's ribs, waist, and abdomen, which allows them to assign the dog a score on a standardized 9-point scale (1 is emaciated, 4-5 is ideal, and 9 is severely obese). In person, scores are reasonably consistent, though a point of disagreement between expert raters is normal. Without palpation, the task becomes more difficult; however, this is an increasingly important skill as telehealth becomes more common in the veterinary field.

In a recent study (Mallikarjun et al., in prep), my colleagues and I asked 1,010 respondents to assign BCS scores to 25 standardized photo sets of different labrador retrievers. Respondents included veterinarians, vet techs, vet students, people who work proximal to the veterinary profession (e.g., veterinary admin staff), and people with no veterinary background. We compared their scores against benchmarks set by sports medicine veterinarians who had hands-on access to the same dogs. We found that scoring accuracy as compared to the benchmarks tracked expertise. Veterinary professionals averaged 0.77 to 0.81 mean absolute error; non-vet respondents averaged 0.90. Further, self-reported confidence did not predict accuracy, but prior exposure to a BCS chart did.

This dataset is excellent for use as an evaluation anchor, due to the standardized stimulus set and a clear expert benchmark established separately by sports medicine veterinarians, alongside a 1000+ value survey with a human distribution large enough to characterize within-group variance and differences between groups. MAE is known for each human group, allowing for comparison to the model performance.

The study

I then ran the same 25-photo task on four frontier vision-language models: Claude Opus 4.7, Claude Sonnet 4.6, GPT-5.5, and GPT-4.1. Each model received the same study instructions human participants saw, the same photographs, and one of two conditions: the Royal Canin 9-point BCS chart included with the prompt, or no chart. This was meant to somewhat align with the question about previous exposure to standardized BCS charts, athough given the ubiquity of BCS charts on the internet it is likely that it is already in most models' training sets of information. Models produced a BCS score for each dog, then in a separate turn assessed certainty of their own rating on the same 1 to 5 Likert scale humans used. Each cell was run multiple times to capture any variability in judgment.

To position models on the gradient, I used a generalized linear mixed-effects model with a Poisson distribution (rater (human groups + individual models) as a fixed effect) and post-hoc contrasts with a Holm correction to directly compare model performance to human performance. This is the same model used in the human-only analysis. Both human respondents and model+condition cells are levels of the rater group factor, with random intercepts for dog (from the photos rated) and rater.

The Model Expertise Gradient

Model performance was then directly compared to human performance.

Graph showing the mean absolute error (MAE) of models as compared to humans with different expertise levels

Claude Opus 4.7 was statistically indistinguishable from every human group, including veterinarians. Both Opus conditions (with BCS chart and without) sat within the veterinary-professional band, with MAE around 0.84.

GPT-5.5 was also statistically indistinguishable from every human group, including veterinarians.

Claude Sonnet 4.6 was significantly worse than every veterinary professional group. It was statistically indistinguishable only from non-vet humans.

GPT-4.1 without the chart had a larger MAE than every human group; however, with the chart, it improved to the level of non-vet humans.

Limitations

The stimulus set is small (25 Labrador retriever photographs from one survey). Visual-only assessment is much more limited than clinical BCS (but is relevant here due to the increase in virtual health appointments). The chart condition tests one specific reference image, and results may differ with other BCS rubrics (although I have briefly checked others and found similar results).

Purpose

The methods here are not specific to BCS or to veterinary medicine. A measured human expertise gradient as the unit of comparison, GLMM positioning with proper multiple-comparison correction, structured failure-mode characterization at specific scale points, and calibration analysis tied to deployment risk: these apply anywhere a visual ordinal scale has an established rubric, an expert benchmark, and a measurable gradient of human expertise.

Clinical analogs include dermatology severity grading (EASI, SCORAD, PASI), radiographic grading (Kellgren-Lawrence, BI-RADS), wound severity scoring, ECOG performance status, and ophthalmologic grading.

What a measured human gradient gives you that a small expert benchmark does not is the ability to make graded statements about the models with specific reference to human performance.
