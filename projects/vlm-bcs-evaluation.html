Evaluating frontier multimodal models against a human expertise gradient: a case study on canine body condition scoring

I evaluated four frontier vision-language models against a measured human expertise gradient on a visual ordinal clinical task, and they sit at distinguishable points on it. The methods generalize to any visual ordinal scale with an established rubric, an expert benchmark, and a measurable gradient of human expertise.

Highlights

  • Claude Opus 4.7: no detected difference from any human group, including veterinary professionals.
  • GPT-5.5: no detected difference from any human group, including veterinary professionals.
  • Claude Sonnet 4.6: significantly worse than every veterinary professional group; performs like the non-veterinary group.
  • GPT-4.1: without reference material, significantly worse than every human group, even the non-veterinary group; with reference material, performs like the non-veterinary group.

Opus 4.7 and GPT-4.1 differ from each other by roughly the same amount as a veterinarian differs from a member of the public. This comparison can be examined specifically because of the dataset providing a human expertise gradient.

Why a measured gradient

Model evaluation against human performance usually takes one of two forms; either the comparison is against a small expert panel, or it is against a large unstratified group of people. This study allows examination of the specific place that different models sit on the human expertise gradient, and how well different models reflect human behavior in the grading task.

I collected a large dataset from experts and non-experts in an ordinal grading task (body condition scoring of dogs), and used this dataset to evaluate four frontier vision-language models. The methods generalize to any visual ordinal clinical scale.

The human anchor

Body condition scoring (BCS) is a routine veterinary assessment. A trained clinician visually examines the dog and palpates the dog's ribs, waist, and abdomen, allowing them to assign a score on a standardized 9-point scale (1 is emaciated, 4-5 is ideal, and 9 is severely obese). In person, scores are reasonably reliable, though a point of disagreement between expert raters is normal. Without palpation, the task becomes more difficult; however, this is an increasingly important skill as telehealth becomes more common in the veterinary field.

In a recent study (Mallikarjun et al., under review), my colleagues and I asked 1,010 respondents to assign BCS scores to 25 standardized Labrador retriever photo sets. Respondents included veterinarians, vet techs, vet students, and people with no veterinary background. We compared their scores against benchmarks set by sports medicine veterinarians who had hands-on access to the same dogs. Accuracy tracked expertise. Veterinary professionals averaged 0.77 to 0.81 mean absolute error; non-vet respondents averaged 0.90. Self-reported confidence did not predict accuracy. Prior exposure to a BCS chart did.

This dataset has the properties an evaluation anchor needs: a fixed stimulus set, an expert benchmark established separately by clinicians with hands-on access, a human distribution large enough that within-group variance is characterized, and an expertise gradient that is measured rather than assumed. MAE is known at each level of veterinary training, which is what most published model evaluations on similar tasks lack; studies often use 1-2 expert graders overall, rather than a full measured expertise gradient.

The study

I ran the same 25-photo task on four frontier vision-language models: Claude Opus 4.7, Claude Sonnet 4.6, GPT-5.5, and GPT-4.1. Each model received the same study instructions human participants saw, the same photographs, and one of two conditions: the Royal Canin 9-point BCS chart included with the prompt, or no chart. Models produced a BCS score, then in a separate turn rated certainty on the same 1 to 5 scale humans used. Each cell was run multiple times.

To position models on the gradient, I extended the Poisson GLMM framework from the human-only analysis. Both human respondents and model+condition cells are levels of the rater group factor, with random intercepts for dog and rater. Incidence rate ratios (IRR) for each model cell against each human group are reported with Holm correction across 32 contrasts. This produces statements like "Opus is indistinguishable from veterinarians at the Holm-corrected threshold" with statistical backing, rather than eyeballed accuracy comparisons.

Note on equivalence: "no detected difference" is not the same as "equivalent." Model cells have smaller effective sample sizes than human groups, so some null contrasts may reflect power rather than true equivalence. Converting "no detected difference" into a proper equivalence claim would require TOST against a pre-specified margin or a Bayes factor for the null. The statements below should be read as "indistinguishable at the Holm-corrected threshold," not as tight equivalence.

What the gradient surfaces: model positioning

The headline result places each model on the measured human gradient.

Figure 1. Each model's MAE plotted against the human expertise gradient, with the veterinary-professional band shaded.

Claude Opus 4.7 was statistically indistinguishable from every human group, including veterinarians (IRR 1.06, Holm p = 1.00 in both chart conditions). Both Opus conditions sat within the veterinary-professional band, with MAE around 0.84.

GPT-5.5 was also statistically indistinguishable from every human group at the Holm-corrected threshold (all p > 0.9), though IRRs of 1.24 to 1.35 against veterinary professionals suggest it likely sits closer to the non-vet end than to vets. The non-significance reflects smaller per-cell sample size for model cells, so this is "no detected difference," not tight equivalence.

Claude Sonnet 4.6 was significantly worse than every veterinary professional group (IRR 1.49 to 1.66; Holm p ≤ 0.03 in the no-chart condition, ≤ 0.006 with chart). It was statistically indistinguishable only from non-vet humans.

GPT-4.1 without the chart was dramatically worse than every human group (IRR 2.16 to 2.50, all Holm p < 0.0001). With the chart it improved but remained significantly worse than veterinary professionals.

What the gradient surfaces: structured failure modes

A gradient anchor also supports characterization of where on the scale models fail, not just whether they fail on average. Three patterns emerged.

Models compress the scale, with the compression concentrated at the high end

Plotting mean rating against the vet benchmark gives a regression slope below 1.0 for every model: Opus 0.64, GPT-5.5 0.59, GPT-4.1 0.57, Sonnet 0.55, against humans at 0.84. Humans show some compression. Models show more, and it concentrates at BCS 8 and 9, where every model except Opus systematically rates overweight and obese dogs as healthier than the benchmark. The deviation at the high end exceeds the human disagreement envelope: humans disagree with the benchmark by roughly a point in either direction, while models disagree by more than a point, consistently downward.

Figure 2. Mean model rating versus the veterinary benchmark, with the human disagreement envelope for comparison.

This is a deployment-relevant failure mode. Canine obesity is already under-recognized by owners, and a tool that systematically downgrades overweight dogs at exactly the point where intervention matters would reinforce that under-recognition. The structural pattern is consistent with the broader pointwise-LLM-evaluation literature, which documents scale compression as a robust phenomenon (Liu et al., 2024), and with the human-medicine analog: Rosario et al. (2024) found GPT-4 concentrated RI-RADS scores on grade C while human raters concentrated on grade D. What the BCS task adds is quantified high-end-specific compression, locatable on a clinical scale where the failure direction has obvious deployment cost.

For Opus, Sonnet, and GPT-5.5, descriptive differences between conditions are within noise; Holm-adjusted contrasts showed no detectable accuracy difference. GPT-4.1 was the exception. Its IRR dropped from 2.16-2.50 against human groups to 1.48-1.71 with the chart, and the chart also collapsed its across-run variance: without the chart, median run-to-run SD on the same item was 2.5 points, and with the chart it dropped to 0. For the weakest model in the comparison, the chart functions as task scaffolding that stabilizes responses.

When asked to articulate the BCS rubric without an image, all four models could describe the 9-point scale in detail. Anthropic models cited Purina/WSAVA; GPT-4.1 cited Laflamme (1997). The underscoring is not due to absence of rubric knowledge. The chart helps the model that needs scaffolding to apply the rubric visually, and does not help the models that already apply it well. This is consistent with what the LLM-as-judge literature has observed about pointwise rating: misalignment lives in how the rubric is applied, not in its prior representation (Liu et al., 2024).

Self-reported confidence is unevenly informative, with one model anti-calibrated

I asked each model to rate certainty on the same 1 to 5 scale used in the human study, in a separate turn from the BCS score.

Figure 3. MAE as a function of self-reported certainty, by model.

Claude Opus showed the cleanest pattern, with MAE dropping from about 1.05 at moderate certainty to about 0.52 at higher certainty. Sonnet and GPT-5.5 showed shallow or flat curves, similar to the human reference (humans in the underlying study also showed no confidence-accuracy correlation). GPT-4.1 is the concerning case: it has the highest average certainty of any model (4.07 of 5, where 4 is "somewhat certain" and 5 is "very certain") and the worst accuracy. High certainty with poor performance also appears in dermatology VLMs (Xu et al., 2025), and it is the deployment-relevant failure mode for any tool that surfaces confidence to a user. A user shown a confident wrong answer has no signal that follow-up is warranted; a user shown a hesitant wrong answer does.

Limitations

The stimulus set is small (25 Labrador retriever photographs from one survey), and repeated model runs on the same items are not statistically independent. Visual-only assessment is more limited than clinical BCS, which includes palpation. The chart condition tests one specific reference image, and results may differ with other BCS rubrics. The GLMM treats each model run as one rater-level observation, which gives model cells smaller effective sample sizes than human groups; some null model-vs-human contrasts may reflect power rather than true equivalence. Statistical indistinguishability from veterinarians is a defensible quantitative statement, not a clinical-equivalence claim.

What this generalizes to

The methods here are not specific to BCS or to veterinary medicine. A measured human expertise gradient as the unit of comparison, GLMM positioning with proper multiple-comparison correction, structured failure-mode characterization at specific scale points, and calibration analysis tied to deployment risk: these apply anywhere a visual ordinal scale has an established rubric, an expert benchmark, and a measurable gradient of human expertise.

Clinical analogs include dermatology severity grading (EASI, SCORAD, PASI), radiographic grading (Kellgren-Lawrence, BI-RADS), wound severity scoring, ECOG performance status, and ophthalmologic grading. The methodology also extends beyond clinical scales: model evaluation on any ordinal judgment task where ground truth is contested or expertise-graded — content moderation severity, code review quality, writing quality ratings, harmfulness classifications — has the same structure. The unit of comparison is a measured human gradient; the question is where on that gradient a model sits and how it fails where it falls short.

What a measured human gradient gives you that a small expert benchmark does not is the ability to make graded, deployment-relevant statements. This model is fit for use at the level of a trained specialist. This model is fit for use at the level of a trainee with supervision. This model surfaces confident wrong answers in a way that makes it unfit for unsupervised screening. Without the gradient, those statements collapse to "model is or is not as good as expert," which is rarely the actual deployment question.

What I'd want to do next with this

The BCS task is a worked example, not the destination. The methodological core — anchoring evaluation in a measured human gradient rather than a small expert panel, characterizing failure modes at specific points on the scale, treating calibration as a deployment-relevant property in its own right — is the contribution that travels. I'd be interested in applying it to evaluation problems where ground truth is contested or expertise-graded: model-graded preference data, expert-vs-crowd disagreement on harm or safety classifications, ordinal judgment tasks where the deployment context spans multiple levels of user expertise, and calibration evaluation tied to the actual cost of a confident wrong answer in a given deployment.
